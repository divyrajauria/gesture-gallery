Playground · Gesture Gallery

Designing a photo gallery you browse with hand gestures, and that still works without a camera.

Point to pick, make an OK sign to grab, pull back to zoom, make a fist to scroll. Recognising a hand was the easy part. The work was everything around it: teaching the gestures in seconds, staying useful when the camera says no, and leaving the model room to run.

Tests drive a simulated hand through every change

It's a Playground piece, so the brief was mine, and I gave it one test: does it convince someone who's skimming in under a minute? Four gestures map to a familiar photo grid. An open palm moves the cursor, pointing taps, an OK sign grabs and moving your hand back enlarges it, and a fist scrolls.

Every decision below was judged against that one-minute test. That's also why some things never got built.

02

What I noticed

Gesture demos lose people before the first gesture.

Most hand-tracking demos assume a webcam, a well-lit room, and someone who already knows the moves. The person I was designing for has none of that guaranteed. They open the link between other tabs, their browser blocks the camera, and nobody has told them what a grab looks like.

Each of those assumptions is a gate, and every gate is a place to lose someone. So I listed the gates first, and gave each one a fix before adding anything new.

03

Reading the hand

Four poses from finger geometry, and one rule that stops accidents.

MediaPipe gives 21 points on the hand in every camera frame. Everything after that is mine. Each fingertip is compared with its middle joint to tell an open palm, a pointing finger and a fist apart. A pose has to hold for about 90 ms before it counts, so the cursor doesn't flicker between modes. A tap is a quick dip of the fingertip, measured against the size of the hand, so slow drift never selects anything.

The natural grab is a pinch. But a hand closing into a fist passes through a pinch on the way, so a plain pinch rule grabs photos whenever someone starts to scroll. I made the grab an OK sign instead: thumb to index, with the other three fingers up, checked on the current frame rather than the smoothed one. It's a less obvious gesture. I accepted that, because an accidental grab costs more than a gesture that takes a moment to learn.

Depth, without a depth camera

Enlarging a photo is "move your hand back". A webcam can't measure distance, but the hand's size in the image is a good stand-in. As the hand gets smaller, the photo grows. Moving sideways drags the photo instead, so the two motions never fight.

04

Teaching it

A tour that waits for you to do the thing.

On a first visit, a small card teaches the four gestures one at a time. It moves on only when the camera sees each one done, because reading about a gesture teaches far less than doing it. Back and Next let people revisit a step, and the full guide can replay the tour at any time.

My first version taught the wrong gesture. The card said "pinch, then pull back towards you", while the code waited for an OK sign moved away from the screen. I only found out by trying it with my own hand. The fix wasn't loosening the detection, which would have brought the accidental grabs back. It was teaching exactly what the code checks, with live hints based on what the camera sees.

The gesture you teach has to be the gesture the code checks for.

05

When the camera says no

Every gesture has a keyboard and mouse twin.

The first build showed a full-screen spinner until hand tracking loaded. If the camera was blocked, that spinner never went away. Now the gallery works from the first second. Tracking loads in the background, and a small card says what's happening.

Each failure gets its own message and a way forward. The library I started with showed a raw browser alert for every camera error, so I request the camera directly instead.

Underneath, the grid is a multi-select listbox with one Tab stop. Arrow keys move, Space selects, Enter enlarges, Esc closes, and selections are announced to screen readers. Reduced-motion settings remove decorative animation but keep hand-driven movement, because for this piece the movement is the interface.

06

Selection that goes somewhere

Point, tap, remove, undo.

Selecting photos used to be a dead end: "3 Selected", then nothing. Now a Remove and Clear bar appears, and an Undo follows every removal. Nothing is really deleted, so it's a safe action to try.

I didn't add a gesture for it. The tap now presses any button under the cursor, and a button lights up when the hand is over it. The buttons are 36 px tall because a hand cursor wobbles more than a mouse, and the header saves room for the bar so the grid doesn't jump when it appears.

07

Light on the machine

Leaving room for the model.

Hand tracking needs a large share of each 33 ms frame. I measured what the page itself was costing, with the processor slowed four times to stand in for an average laptop. With nobody touching it, the page was spending about 14 ms per frame on nothing.

The causes were small and invisible. A loading spinner kept animating after its card was hidden, which forced a repaint every frame. All 200 photos were promoted to their own layers, so each repaint touched every one. The hover check measured every tile instead of asking the browser once. And the model ran on every screen refresh, about 60 times a second, though a webcam only delivers about 30 frames.

Switching tabs now releases the camera, so its light goes off, and it reconnects quietly when you come back. I checked that one in a real browser window with real tabs, because the testing tool keeps every page "visible" and couldn't prove it.

Idle should cost nothing. Most of the fixes were deleting work.

08

Proving it without a webcam

58 tests move a simulated hand through the real page.

A test server can't wave at a camera. So the tests swap the tracking model for a stand-in that feeds hand positions frame by frame, and the page's own gesture code runs exactly as it would with a real hand. They cover every gesture, keyboard and mouse, every camera failure, the tour and its hints, Remove and Undo, the offline photo fallback, and reduced motion.

To check the tests were real, I deliberately broke the OK-sign rule. The matching test failed straight away. They now run on every pull request, and a change that breaks a gesture can't reach the live site unnoticed.

09

How I worked

I set the direction, an AI pair wrote much of the code, and my own hand was the final test.

I worked one change at a time. Each one was proposed, built on a branch, checked against the test suite, and tried on my own camera before it went live. The simulations caught most problems. My hand caught the one they couldn't: a tour teaching a different gesture from the one the code checked for.

Scope came from the one-minute test. A calibration indicator ("too close, too far") was on the list, and I dropped it, because the tour's live hints already cover the main confusion. Mobile support and new gestures stayed out for the same reason: neither changes how someone skimming judges the piece.

11

What I'd change, and what's next

The performance numbers come from a slowed-down browser, not a real mid-range laptop, and they leave out the model's own cost. That's the next thing I'd measure, on borrowed hardware, before calling it fast.

I'd also bring in other people's hands earlier. Every gesture threshold was tuned on mine, so the next round is a few people, a few lighting conditions, and a few minutes each, watching where the hints fire.
